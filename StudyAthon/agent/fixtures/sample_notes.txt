Cell Biology - Lecture 3: Mitosis and the Cell Cycle

Mitosis is the process by which a single cell divides to produce two genetically
identical daughter cells. Before a cell divides, it must replicate its DNA so
that each daughter cell receives a complete copy of the genome.

The cell cycle has four phases: G1, S, G2, and M. In G1 the cell grows and
performs normal metabolic functions. During S phase, DNA replication occurs and
the chromosome number is unchanged, though the DNA content doubles. In G2 the
cell continues to grow and prepares the machinery needed for division. The M
phase is mitosis itself, followed by cytokinesis.

Mitosis proceeds through four stages, defined by what happens to the
chromosomes. In prophase, chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes, the
nuclear envelope begins to break down, and the mitotic spindle forms from
centrosomes at opposite poles of the cell. In metaphase, chromosomes align along
the cell's equator, or metaphase plate, and each chromosome attaches to spindle
microtubules at its centromere. In anaphase, sister chromatids separate and are
pulled to opposite poles; this is the point at which the cell is temporarily
containing four nuclei rather than one. In telophase, a nuclear envelope reforms
around each set of chromosomes and the chromosomes begin to decondense.

Mitosis is genetically conservative with one important exception: telophase is
accompanied by cytokinesis, the division of the cytoplasm, producing two
separate cells. Cytokinesis in animal cells proceeds by cleavage, in which a
contractile ring of actin filaments and myosin pinches the cell in two.

Common misconceptions worth noting: chromosomes are not the same thing as
chromatids, since a chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at the
centromere after S phase. Also, DNA content doubles during S phase, but the
chromosome count does not, because a replicated chromosome is still counted as a
single chromosome.
